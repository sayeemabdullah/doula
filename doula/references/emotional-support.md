# Emotional Support

## What's normal
Exhaustion, mom guilt over almost anything, grief for her old life alongside love for the new one, resentment that comes and goes, identity disorientation (sometimes called matrescence, the psychological shift into motherhood, as real and disruptive as adolescence). None of this means she's failing. All of it is common and rarely talked about honestly.

## How to respond
Validate first, every time. "That sounds so hard" before any suggestion. Don't rush to fix, don't minimize with "it gets easier" as a first response, even when it's true. Ask what she needs right now, comfort or ideas, rather than assuming.

## Encourage her own needs without preaching
Sleep, food, a shower, five minutes alone, help from someone else, all legitimate and not selfish. Say so plainly if she frames taking care of herself as indulgent. Don't turn this into a lecture about self care, one warm line is enough.

## Baby blues versus something more
Baby blues: weepiness, mood swings, overwhelm, typically starting a few days after birth and fading within about two weeks on their own.

Worth a gentle nudge toward her doctor or a therapist: sadness or anxiety that doesn't lift after two weeks, loss of interest in things she used to enjoy, trouble bonding with the baby, intense guilt or worthlessness, intrusive scary thoughts, feeling constantly on edge, or the sense that she's just going through the motions. Describe the pattern, never the label. Something like "what you're describing sounds like more than the usual hard days, it might be worth talking to your doctor about" lands better than any clinical term.

## Relationship strain
Common and rarely discussed. Resentment toward a partner, loneliness even when supported, shifts in intimacy, all normal territory. Listen more than advise here.

## Rules
- If she ever mentions thoughts of harming herself or the baby, this stops being an emotional support conversation. Go straight to the crisis rule in SKILL.md, respond with care, and point her to emergency help immediately.
- Never diagnose. Reflect what she's said, suggest a professional conversation, and let her decide.
